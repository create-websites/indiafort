Sikkim

Kingdom of the Himalayas

Explore a unique heritage of royal capitals, grand Buddhist monasteries, and an architectural style shaped by faith and mountains.

Royal Seats of Power

Sikkim's history is defined by the Chogyal dynasty. Its "forts" were primarily royal capitals that served as the political and spiritual centers of the kingdom, rather than purely military structures.

Rabdentse Ruins

The second capital of the Kingdom of Sikkim from 1670 to 1814, Rabdentse was a grand palace complex. Today, its evocative ruins, maintained by the ASI, offer a glimpse into the kingdom's glorious past. The site includes the remains of the palace, chortens (shrines), and a stone throne, all with a stunning backdrop of the Kanchenjunga range. [6, 7, 8, 9]

Tsuklakhang Palace, Gangtok

The royal palace and monastery in Gangtok, the final capital of Sikkim. Built in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, it served as the site for coronations, marriages, and victory ceremonies for the royal family. Its architecture is a beautiful example of the traditional Tibetan style, with a distinctive pagoda-like gate. [10, 11, 12, 13]

Sikkimese Monastery Architecture

The architecture of Sikkim is dominated by its Buddhist monasteries (*gompas*). This style is heavily influenced by Tibetan traditions but has unique adaptations suited to the Himalayan climate and local materials.

Tibetan Style with Sloping Roofs

While the core design follows Tibetan principles, Sikkimese monasteries feature sloping roofs, often multi-tiered, to cope with the heavy monsoon rains and snowfall, a key difference from the flat roofs common in Tibet. [14, 15]

Intricate Woodwork & Murals

A hallmark of the style is the extensive use of wood for pillars, doors, and windows, all adorned with intricate carvings of dragons and other religious motifs. The interiors are covered in vibrant murals depicting scenes from Buddhist mythology. [14, 16]

Stupas and Chortens

These sacred monuments are integral to the landscape. The Do Drul Chorten in Gangtok is a prime example, a large stupa built in 1945 to pacify the spirits of the land, surrounded by 108 prayer wheels for devotees to spin. [17, 18, 19]

Key Monasteries & Monuments

Sikkim is dotted with hundreds of monasteries, each with its own unique history and significance. These are some of the most important spiritual and architectural centers in the state.

Rumtek Monastery

The largest monastery in Sikkim, Rumtek is the seat of the Kagyu school of Tibetan Buddhism in exile. Rebuilt in the 1960s, it is a replica of the original in Tibet and houses some of the rarest Buddhist religious art and scriptures in the world. [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]

Pemayangtse Monastery

Meaning "Perfect Sublime Lotus," this is one of the oldest and most premier monasteries in Sikkim, founded in 1647. It follows the Nyingma order and its head lama had the unique privilege of anointing the Chogyal kings. The three-storied structure is famed for its sculptures and paintings. [25, 26, 27, 28]

Dubdi Monastery

Known as the oldest monastery in Sikkim, Dubdi was established in 1701. Its name means "the hermit's cell." Perched on a hilltop above Yuksom, the first capital, this two-story stone structure is a site of immense historical importance for Sikkimese Buddhism. [20, 29, 30]

When to Visit

Sikkim's climate varies with its altitude. The best times to visit for clear mountain views and pleasant weather are during the spring and autumn months.

Peak Season (March - May & Oct - Nov)

Spring and autumn offer pleasant weather and clear skies, ideal for sightseeing, trekking, and getting the best views of the Himalayan peaks. [9, 20]

Monsoon Season (June - September)

The monsoon brings heavy rainfall, which can lead to landslides and disrupt travel. However, the landscape is incredibly lush and green during this time.

Winter Season (December - February)

Winters are cold, with heavy snowfall in the higher altitudes. This season is perfect for those who want to experience a snowy Himalayan landscape.
